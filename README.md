# Evidence Infrastructure

Food and agricultural decisions depend on decision-makers trusting the evidence base they use to act. Data are one input into that evidence base, but trust depends, in part, on how decisions about those data are made. This project examines how Colorado's food and agricultural decision-makers use research evidence, which sources they trust, and what happens to that trust when the public data underlying the evidence are changed or discontinued.

In September 2025, USDA announced the termination of future Household Food Security Reports, describing the studies as redundant and costly (USDA, 2025). The underlying Current Population Survey Food Security Supplement had provided a national measure of food insecurity for 30 years (USDA ERS, 2026). Its discontinuation raises questions about the evidence available to evaluate food and nutrition policy, as well as the process through which decisions about that evidence are made. Modernizing federal statistical infrastructure requires decisions about what to retain, revise, or replace. When those decisions proceed without consulting the communities that rely on the data, what uses are overlooked, what research becomes infeasible, and what happens to trust in the institutions responsible?

The project builds on work with Julia Lane's Democratizing Data team, which uses publication metadata to document how public datasets are used in research, including food and agriculture (Democratizing Data, n.d.). This work provides a way to identify research communities whose dependence on a dataset may otherwise be difficult to observe. The availability of that information raises a further question: when users are not consulted, is their use of the data not visible, or is the evidence they produce not considered credible or relevant to the decision? Understanding the distinction matters because making data use more visible may not resolve a problem rooted in distrust of the evidence or the people producing it.

Colorado provides a setting for examining these relationships. State agencies, producer organizations, and policy offices draw on research and public data to make decisions about food and agriculture. Understanding what evidence they use, how they judge its credibility, and where access limits its use is a starting point for identifying how trust in institutions is built and where it is most vulnerable.

## Research questions

The guiding question is: **Who do food and agricultural decision-makers in Colorado trust, and what would it take to strengthen that trust?** The project addresses five related questions:

1. **Evidence use and access.** What research evidence do decision-makers rely on, where does it come from, and how does it enter their decisions? Where does data access itself, apart from trust, limit their use of evidence?
2. **Visibility of user communities.** When datasets are changed or discontinued without consulting their users, is this because the communities and their uses are not visible to those making the decision? Which uses are documented, and which remain unrecognized?
3. **Credibility of evidence.** When users and their work are visible, is the evidence they produce trusted and considered relevant? What makes decision-makers accept, question, or discount that evidence, and whose expertise carries weight?
4. **Consultation and the evidence base.** How are user communities involved in decisions to retain, revise, or discontinue data? When they are not consulted, what are the consequences for the research, policy evaluation, and practical decisions that depend on those data?
5. **Trust in data and institutions.** How does the process of changing public data affect users' confidence in the data and in the institutions that produce them? Where has trust broken down, and what forms of engagement, transparency, or continuity would help strengthen it?

## Approach and intended contribution

The proposed first phase consists of 15 to 20 interviews with Colorado state agencies, producer organizations, and policy offices, following development of an interview protocol and IRB approval. Interviews will document the evidence participants use, the sources they trust and why, their experiences with changes in data availability, and their opportunities to communicate their needs. The analysis will distinguish barriers to accessing evidence from doubts about its credibility and experiences of exclusion from decisions about data.

The intended output is a report mapping where research evidence enters Colorado food and agricultural policy and where trust in institutions is strong, weak, or absent. Interviews will document participants' experiences and explanations; they will not, on their own, establish why a federal agency made a particular decision or the causal effect of that decision on trust.

The College of Agricultural Sciences already has relationships with stakeholders through Extension, research, and engagement. This project can inform how the College and the Agricultural Experiment Station use those relationships to communicate the value of research evidence, identify needs that are not being heard, and connect data producers with the communities that depend on their work. The broader objective is to understand what sustains an evidence base that food and agricultural decision-makers can use and trust.

This project is in development. Methods and findings will be added as the research progresses.

**Project lead:** Lauren Chenarides, Associate Professor, Department of Agricultural and Resource Economics, Colorado State University.
